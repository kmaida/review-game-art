# Review game art

Judge the asset against its intended gameplay role, the brief, and the project's art direction. Use the project's documented cameras, accessibility requirements, asset specifications, and review workflow when available. Give visual appeal and believable function equal standing in the review. Compare approved assets from the same family for in-game consistency when available. Treat visible artifacts as quality findings. Record provenance from source files, work history, known tool use, or verified credentials when available. Leave origin unknown if the evidence is inconclusive. Surface style, mesh density, detector scores, and missing metadata are insufficient to establish origin.

## Find references before designing or judging unfamiliar details

- Start with the feature that needs an answer, such as how a strut seats or which joints move when an animal turns. Look for actual photographs, measured drawings, anatomy diagrams, manufacturer diagrams or CAD, and footage of the movement in question. Seek a second angle, section, or key poses when the important detail is hidden.
- Trace each reference to its original photographer, archive, maker, or published drawing. Use documented non-AI-generated sources for appearance and construction whenever available. Skip generated imagery and images of unknown origin as evidence of how something is built or works. If an exact match is unavailable, use the closest documented physical example, state what you inferred, and work it out in the concept before verifying it in 3D.
- Record the source link, the relevant page or view, and what it informs in the brief or review. Adapt the reference's principles and details to the game's visual style. See [reference examples](references/physical-references.md) for structural joins, industrial architecture, and moving assemblies.

## Approve the final concept before modeling

Approve the final 2D concept, including the exact artwork for **every required gameplay state**, before creating a mesh, starting a 3D blockout, or submitting artwork to an image-to-3D generator.

- **Finish the design.** Resolve silhouette, proportions, visual hierarchy, materials, detail, and visual identity. Check readability from the intended game view and inspect the full-size artwork for artifacts and unfinished areas.
- **Show and approve every required state in 2D.** List the states from the gameplay brief and show each one in the concept art. Include states conveyed through material, lighting, or effects, such as inactive, active, depleted, damaged, and wrecked states where the brief calls for them. For staged construction, show each planned visible stage and what the permanent and temporary parts do. Show key moments when a transition is itself a gameplay cue. Check that each cue reads at its intended game scale. When color communicates gameplay, use the project's required color-vision simulations and confirm that shape, value, pattern, or motion also carries the meaning. Review and record approval for each required state as part of the final concept.
- **Resolve the construction.** Show intended world dimensions and footprint, with consistent proportions, alignment, part fit, supports, and continuity across the views the modeler needs. Use callouts, sections, or extra views for hidden features that affect the build. For mechanisms, show the moving and fixed parts, pivots or guides, key poses, and intended travel or clearance in 2D.
- **Approve a construction pose for rigged or segmented organic assets.** Include a neutral pose or the intended rigging rest pose, drawn with minimal foreshortening. Show full limb and appendage lengths, joint spacing, and attachment points. Use additional views or callouts to reveal folded or overlapping parts, such as an extended wing with its attachment visible.
- **Approve organic movement clearance in 2D.** Use key poses, overlays, or sections to check how neighboring anatomy, horns, shells, armor, clothing, or foliage fit through the intended motion. Include extremes and intermediate poses where clearance is tight. Resolve obstructions and unintended intersections before any modeling starts.
- **Review the actual handoff.** Follow the project's art-review and approval process. Resolve open design decisions and record the approved concept version, state artwork, and supporting views or callouts. A **Revise** or **Incomplete** verdict on any required state, construction pose, or clearance check keeps the entire asset in 2D.

## Design and review at the right stage

1. **Concept.** State what the player must recognize, from which view, and which shapes communicate it. Check the silhouette and large value groups at the intended display size with textures, markings, and glow removed. Compare with related assets and reconcile alternate views before approval.
2. **Image and texture.** Inspect at delivery size for readability and at 100% for artifacts. For cutouts, check edges against light and dark backgrounds. For sets, compare views, frames, or variants side by side. Apply the organic motion checks to animated organic assets. Tile repeatable textures across a representative level span to spot obvious repeating landmarks and value patterns. Examine PBR maps independently when supplied.
3. **3D model.** Compare the blockout or first generated mesh with the approved concept views. If the result departs from the approved design in silhouette, support layout, motion axis, gameplay state cues, or other substantial details, correct the mesh or approve revised artwork in 2D before continuing in 3D. Verify world dimensions and footprint against the brief using a trusted scale reference in the same scene. Check that visible scale cues such as doors, rungs, and fasteners suit their intended use. Orbit the mesh in neutral lighting, including top, back, and underside when relevant. Inspect the solid form and the lit model. Use wireframe, UVs, and material maps to diagnose issues. Apply the organic or mechanical motion checks as appropriate and test state and LOD transitions when present.
4. **Gameplay.** Inspect the asset in the game or the project's runtime preview, using the actual scene or UI background. Review the intended cameras and the full range of viewing distances, including any close inspection view the game provides. Repeat the required accessibility checks in context and keep the evidence with the project's QA records.
   - **Busy scenes.** Check that the asset's role, facing, identity, and required states read by silhouette, value, pattern, or motion in typical and visually busy play. Include nearby units, overlapping scenery, and effects where they affect readability. Check that states such as construction stages stay distinguishable during those moments.
   - **Gameplay bounds.** Compare the rendered footprint and implied access with collision, selection, interaction, or pathing bounds where applicable. Use debug overlays when available and exercise the relevant actions in-game. Flag mismatches such as a walkable-looking stair that is blocked or decorative roots that create an invisible wall.
   - **Sprite placement and facing.** Check that sprite pivots and depth-sort anchors correspond to the intended ground contact or other attachment. Play animations and switch between supported facings near scenery. Look for height or volume jumps, planted contacts sliding, or sprites drawing in the wrong order as they pass behind or in front of objects.

## Review your own work before handoff

1. **List what needs coverage.** Use the approved concept and the actual asset or runtime sequence. For a set, cover each asset and any variant that changes the result. For a staged build, list every visible stage and transition, including parts that appear only during assembly.
2. **Collect evidence and compare it.** Use the available local preview and capture tools to check the actual deliverable. Compare textured or material-driven assets directly to their approved concepts using the [material and texture checks](references/material-review.md). For staged assets, review the complete motion and inspect each stage individually using the [construction sequence checks](references/construction-review.md). Apply every relevant art and technical check to each stage, with explicit checks for symmetry, part purpose, fit, supports, and clearance.
3. **Fix and recheck before presenting a pass.** When making the asset, fix the defects within the approved design and task scope. Capture the revised result and recheck the affected views, stages, and transitions. For a staged set, keep a compact record of each asset, stage, and transition with the evidence and verdict. A batch passes when each applicable check has evidence and passes. Use **Incomplete** for required checks that still lack evidence.

## Visual appeal and detail

- **Make the whole design hold together.** Start in concept art. Judge the silhouette, proportions, balance of solids and open space, and the relationship between the primary mass and smaller forms. Check what draws the eye first and what gives the design its character. Compare related assets in the same view and lighting to catch distinct designs that share the same outline under different colors. If the direction feels generic or awkward, test a different proportion, silhouette, or arrangement of major forms before committing to the finish.
- **Give detail a hierarchy.** At gameplay scale, the major forms and defining accents should read clearly. In close views, reward inspection with well-shaped edges, clean joins, convincing material changes, and details that give the asset character and scale. Look for unfinished areas and detail that crowds the focal point, such as surface cuts, wear, or glow spread evenly across the design. An industrial machine can carry welds, stencils, and wear around selected seams while keeping a broad panel visually quiet.
- **Use style deliberately.** Ornament, unusual proportions, and expressive asymmetry can give a design character. Check how they reinforce the visual rhythm, silhouette, or visual identity and how they fit around the structure and moving parts. Judge the finished asset under intended lighting and alongside its neighbors. Describe aesthetic findings by naming the design choice and what would improve it.

## UI art checklist

- **Hierarchy and legibility.** Review at actual gameplay or menu size. Check that critical information is easy to spot and that type, numerals, and labels read over the intended backgrounds, including busy scenes behind a HUD.
- **Icons and styling.** Compare similar icons at their smallest supported size. Check that silhouettes are easy to distinguish and that line weight, proportions, and detail feel consistent with the interface's visual style.
- **State cues.** Compare focus, hover, pressed, selected, disabled, and warning states where used. Check that each state reads clearly and that important distinctions also use shape, value, pattern, or a label.
- **Visual fit.** At supported sizes and aspect ratios, check that key elements stay within the safe area. Look for clipped text, colliding labels, stretched corners or borders on sliced panels, and artwork that covers important content. Include real strings, with longer localized labels where supported.

For a full HUD or menu review, pair this checklist with `game-ui-ux` when installed to assess navigation, input, scaling, and screen flow. In a standalone installation, use the project brief to check visual layout and scaling. Report any interaction checks that still need evidence.

## Organic anatomy and motion

- **Establish the anatomy in 2D.** For people, animals, and fictional creatures, use relevant anatomy diagrams and observed movement. Show how the spine, limbs, digits, wings, or tails attach and which joints, sections, or tissues move. For plants, show where leaves and branches attach and how they bend or unfold. Check proportions and attachment points across views and required state poses. A stylized or invented body can follow its own anatomy and movement rules. Establish those rules in the concept and apply them consistently.
- **Check natural articulation and continuity.** Trace each moving part back to the body through the full action. Check where joints bend and rotate and how neighboring parts share the motion. Flag unintended separation, fused or extra parts, motion at the wrong joint, abrupt changes in limb length or volume, and skin that collapses or twists around the joint. Recheck the approved clearances in the delivered animation, including rigid parts that bend unexpectedly or pass through the body. In a still, check the visible anatomy and ask for another view when the attachment is hidden.
- **Verify motion in the delivered asset.** Watch at normal speed for weight and rhythm, then step through contact, release, extreme poses, loop seams, and transitions. Check stance feet or paws, grips, and other points of support. Check visible facial movement and breathing, anchored bases, and how appendages follow and settle. Compare 2D frames with consistent registration and inspect the posed 3D skeleton and surface together. Test the combinations of movement and actions that can occur in gameplay. Use the [organic asset checks and reference examples](references/organic-assets.md) for anatomy, rigging, and species-specific motion.

## Alignment, symmetry, and fit

Check these in the concept and verify them in the blockout or first mesh after concept approval. Recheck after cleanup or export when the geometry changes. In images, use perspective guides and compare corresponding edges in the same object plane. For models, use orthographic views aligned to the part’s local axes and measure the source geometry when a mismatch is hard to judge by eye.

- **Alignment.** Trace the intended centerlines, edges, and planes. Check for off-center openings, tilted hardware, stepped seams, uneven spacing, and holes that miss their pins or fasteners. Confirm that elements meant to be flush, parallel, or coaxial share the intended plane or axis.
- **Symmetry and repetition.** Compare mirrored or repeated parts against the intended axis, plane, or radial spacing. Check matching counts, profiles, dimensions, offsets, angles, and gaps. A mirrored overlay or aligned side-by-side view can reveal small drift, such as one column leaning or a matching bracket missing. Compare intentional offsets or variations against the brief and how the assembly works.
- **Continuity behind obstructions.** Trace both visible ends of a rail, pipe, seam, trim line, or other continuous feature through the hidden span. In an image, use perspective guides to check that the reappearing segment matches the expected position, width, direction, and curvature. For example, the two ends of a straight rail behind a housing should align on the same axis in perspective. In 3D, hide the obstruction or look from behind to find gaps, offset segments, and abrupt changes in profile. At a deliberate splice or transition, check that the pieces actually meet and follow the intended path.
- **Mating parts.** Inspect the contact surfaces in the seated or closed state. For a box and lid, trace the lid’s mating lip against the box rim, including the corners. Check that their profiles and dimensions allow the intended clearance or overhang and that the lid seats at the right height. For a single image with hidden contact surfaces, flag visible mismatches and identify the angle or state needed to confirm the fit.
- **Modular joins.** Assemble a representative run of kit pieces at level scale. Exercise straight joins, corners, and supported rotations or flips with their actual neighbors. Inspect the joins for geometry gaps, trim or pattern breaks, and shading discontinuities. Check the assembled span for distracting repetition and compare any deliberate seams with the concept.

### Close-up joint fit

- **Inspect both ends of braces and struts.** Orbit the assembled joint in an untextured solid view. Check the outside face, underside, and silhouette from a shallow angle. Isolate the parts or use a section view if you can’t see where an end lands. Look for ends poking through a neighboring surface, excessive overhang, uneven gaps, stepped faces, pinched bevels, and flicker from overlapping faces.
- **Fit the end to the receiving member.** Trim, miter, or shape the end to follow the mating profile as the design calls for. Check that contact and any visible seam, weld, collar, or overhang read as intentional. Buried overlap is acceptable when the visible faces, silhouette, and motion are clean. Recheck repaired joints on the finished export with materials at close zoom.
- **Braced frame example.** Check that a mitered rail reads as a continuous frame and that diagonal struts seat cleanly against it. Inspect from inside and outside the frame to catch ends that protrude past the opposite face or corner.

## Architectural placement and structure

- **Trace the support.** In concept drawings and again in the blockout, follow the major loads from roofs, platforms, or heavy equipment through beams, posts, and connections to a base. Check where both ends of each member land. A vertical post should stand plumb and meet its beam and footing. Flag a post that kinks, leans into a panel, or misses its connection. A diagonal brace makes sense when it ties real connection points together and stabilizes the frame. Use front and side views to confirm the angle and connection points.
- **Check what actually carries a stacked or mounted part.** In plan and side views, compare the upper part’s contact points and apparent weight with the size and position of its base, cradle, or mount. Check that feet or pads seat on the support and that the assembly looks stable. Flag a cabinet balanced on an undersized or offset pedestal, feet hanging beyond a platform, or a hidden gap at an apparent contact. For a deliberate overhang or cantilever, show the connection or counterbalance that makes it believable.
- **Check placement against use.** Trace the intended route to doors, platforms, controls, and service panels. Look for a stair that misses its landing, a column in the middle of a bay opening, or a control placed beyond reach. Check that repeated bays and openings line up with the frame and that departures have a clear purpose such as terrain, access, equipment, or a readable design accent.
- **Seat the asset on the intended terrain.** Check terrain-placed assets across their supported slopes and ground variations in game. Verify that foundations or feet make the intended contact and that doors, stairs, vents, and service openings stay clear of the ground.
- **Keep room for style.** Use inclined frames, arches, asymmetrical wings, ornamental ribs, and surface detail where they suit the art direction. Check that the major connections and access routes still read with the detail removed, and that the detail follows the form or gives it a deliberate accent.

## Mechanical function in 3D

For each assembly meant to move, identify what stays fixed, what moves, what guides and drives it, and how it starts and stops. Establish the motion in the concept’s drawings, then test it on the first 3D assembly and verify it on the finished geometry.

- **Joints and supports.** Check that the actual pivot or slide axis matches the modeled hardware in 3D. A hinge attaches to both members, its knuckles share a pin axis, and its moving leaf turns with the panel. A sliding part stays engaged with its guides. Check linkage travel, end stops, and any hoses or cables that must follow the motion.
- **Handles and latches.** Check that the handle is attached to the intended part, can be reached and gripped from the working side, and has hand clearance where it is used. Confirm that the latch meets its catch when closed and releases before the part moves. Check the handle and latch’s own travel and their clearance around nearby geometry.
- **Clearance through the full path.** Pose or animate the assembled mesh with its actual pivots and limits. Inspect both endpoints and the intervening motion, including combined movements and neighboring attachments where they can interfere. Use a swept-volume or mesh intersection check where practical. For tight fits, measure the closest gap and inspect more closely around the smallest clearances. Allow intended contact at seats, bearings, and stops. Check the exported motion in the game or lab if the runtime drives it.
- **Compatible attachments.** Check the supported attachment envelope against the project's mount specifications or game rules. Exercise the largest and tightest supported combinations through their combined movements, including extreme poses and any points of closest approach.
- **Ground contact and weight in motion.** Watch the exported motion at play scale and in close views where available. For walkers, check that stance feet stay planted until lift-off and that cadence, footfalls, and body motion convey the intended weight. Check relevant turns, starts, and stops as well as steady movement.

- **Hinged box.** Release the latch, grip the handle, and follow the lid through the full arc. Check that the back lip clears the rim just after lift-off, the handle clears nearby parts, and the catch lines up when the lid reseats.
- **Rail-mounted carriage.** Check that the rails align with the carriage’s guides and support it through the full stroke. Sweep the entire carriage, including rollers, overhangs, and any payload. Look for bolt heads, brackets, pipes, cables, or covers that cross its path. If a cover or gate must open for travel, check that its motion clears the path before the carriage reaches it. Confirm that the carriage reaches its intended stops without binding or leaving its guides.

A finished mechanism passes only with 3D evidence of fit and clearance through its intended motion. During concept review, use 2D sections, key poses, or travel diagrams to resolve uncertainties before handing off to 3D. When reviewing a finished model from stills, request a posed view or motion preview if clearance needs confirmation.

## What else to look for

| Signal | How to check it |
|---|---|
| **Forms that merge or change identity** | Look for unexplained dead ends, pipes that taper into unrelated parts, melted joins, extra or fused anatomy where applicable, and inconsistent counts or proportions across views or frames. |
| **Markings and lighting that break the scene** | Read stencils and symbols at source size. Look for pseudo-text, phantom markings, mismatched perspective, conflicting shadows or reflections, and objects that appear to float where they should make contact. Check against the intended lighting and style. |
| **A mesh designed around one view** | Compare the front and back in solid view. Look for flattened side profiles, a duplicated front on the back, missing undersides, and detail that disappears from certain angles. Distinguish geometry the silhouette or motion requires from detail deliberately baked into a texture. |
| **Surfaces that fall apart when relit** | Rotate the light and inspect the maps. Look for painted highlights or directional shadows fighting the scene lighting, smeared UV projections and seams, roughness that ignores material boundaries, and relief that breaks around corners. Compare deliberate baked shading against the art direction and behavior in game. |
| **Geometry that fails in use** | Look for floating fragments, accidental holes or fused shells, wavering planes, and tangled triangles where they cause shading, motion, collision, or budget problems. Move parts to reveal clipping, false openings, or joints that visually tear. Check against the asset’s actual budget and expected use. |

Use the project's visual logic when interpreting a cue. A suspended machine may need visible supports, and a floating object can be believable in a setting that establishes how it moves and behaves. Repetition, asymmetry, and simplified geometry can serve the approved style. Check that each treatment reads consistently across views and states.

## Verdict and handoff

- **Pass for the stage** when the design is visually appealing, appropriately detailed for its purpose and stage, and meets the brief, gameplay read, and applicable functional and technical checks. A concept pass authorizes 3D modeling only when the recorded 2D artwork for every required gameplay state has passed.
- **Revise** when weak proportions, unconvincing materials, sparse or cluttered detail, or a functional defect undermines the asset. Resolve the largest design and function problems before refining surfaces and effects. Recheck affected views and states after a fix.
- **Incomplete** when evidence needed for approval is missing. Give the findings the supplied material supports and name the view, source, or test that would resolve the gap. A good render can establish the look from that view. Mesh, materials, and motion need their own evidence.

Lead with the stage verdict and prioritize findings by impact. For each finding, identify the view, zoom, or frame and the specific part, state what fails, and give a concrete fix and recheck. Report any checks still pending. For example, “From the gameplay camera the struts disappear into the background and the pod looks unsupported. Adjust the struts' value contrast and recheck them against the level lighting.”

For final review, tie the verdict to the exact export or build tested. Record the reviewed views and zooms, states and configurations where applicable, the references that informed the design, and the results of the project's applicable technical checks. For 3D assets these may include sockets, pivots, transforms, bounds, budgets, and materials. Mark the review **Incomplete** if evidence required for the verdict is still missing.
